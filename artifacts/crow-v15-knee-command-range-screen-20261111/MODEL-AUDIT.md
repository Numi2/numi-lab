# Current model and the observed stiff gait

The v15 journey host constructs its body from the American crow active-head model, adds two hinged segments to each toe, and inserts a roll joint at each hip. It does not subdivide the torso into articulated or deformable segments. The torso root nevertheless has six rigid-body degrees of freedom; a rigid torso is not an immovable torso. Torso flexion is absent, while root sway and pitch depend on dynamics and controls. The source contains modeled angular damping and estimated masses and joint geometry, not same-specimen measurements.

The parent knee position drives are configured with stiffness 65 N m/rad, damping 0.50 N m s/rad and maximum drive effort 0.75 N m. The walking supervisor also limits the knee swing action coefficient to 0.40 and multiplies it by the 0.80 gait scale; with the 0.75-rad action scale, the full requested action range is approximately 0.24 rad before other gating. Thus the model combines stiff tracking with a restricted swing request; either can affect the appearance. The independent softened-drive screen in ../crow-v15-knee-compliance-screen-20261111 rejected a simple reduction in stiffness.

In retained seed 20261127 lane 5, accepted replay frames 200–299 show left and right knee spans of 0.2330 and 0.2333 rad, root-height span 0.00183 m, and root roll and pitch spans of approximately 2.60 and 1.06 degrees. These are two seconds of one used development start, not anatomical targets. The body-following renderer removes root translation and estimates skinning, feather surfaces and toe bridges; inspect the native pose and contact trace as well as the video.

The default source material has static/dynamic friction fields 0.8/0.6, copied to the ground and perch. The contact path combines two materials by geometric mean. No specimen-specific crow foot or perch measurement is established. The separate contact-friction screen tests a material change without claiming it resolves grasp geometry or control.

## Anatomical constraint on interpretation

A rigid torso is an approximation, but adding an arbitrarily flexible mammal-like spine would not by itself make a crow more realistic. Avian spinal fusion varies across taxa, and fused thoracic vertebrae have evolved repeatedly among songbirds (James, 2009, https://repository.si.edu/items/41d113ed-0574-49a7-ace5-28efb37fa4c6). Three-dimensional quail walking research describes coordinated trunk and thigh motion (Abourachid et al., 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21982408/). These are not crow-specific kinematic calibration data. Prioritize state-driven body and limb coordination and identify the crow-specific anatomical evidence before introducing new torso joints; retain the current model limitation explicitly.
