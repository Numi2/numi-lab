# American-crow burst takeoff: primary reference

Jackson and Dial (2011), “Scaling of mechanical power output during burst escape flight in the Corvidae”, Journal of Experimental Biology 214:452–461, https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.046789 ([author-posted text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49748898_Scaling_of_mechanical_power_output_during_burst_escape_flight_in_the_Corvidae)). The study includes three American crows, with reported body mass 0.3876 ± 0.0315 kg (table 1). During burst takeoff and vertical escape, the table reports 6.4 ± 0.1 Hz mean wingbeat frequency (SEM), maximum 7.1 Hz; wingbeats after the first downstroke were pooled. These are limited-sample, maneuver-specific observations, not a cruise target or an American-crow population range. Pectoralis shortening duty is a muscle measure, not a directly interchangeable wing-angle duty.

For the American crow, the paper reports onset of the initial downstroke before toe-off; the magpie in that study started its downstroke after toe-off. This direct species observation makes simultaneous wing/leg coordination worth testing, without treating the magpie timing as an American-crow rule. Model observables should record wing trajectories and toe normal impulses at the same native substeps, separately from leg-generated ground impulse, launch velocity and complete-sequence outcomes. The model does not measure pectoralis strain or force, so it cannot establish muscle agreement.

The retained early-wing observer measured downward wing-center motion while feet were loaded, but the early-wing candidate lost leg push and the sequence failed supported landing. The later-gated candidate retained leg impulse without establishing this pre-toe-off downstroke. Neither is qualified. The earlier 4.6 Hz candidate used a different species and a tail-acceleration proxy during flight; it cannot be compared directly to this burst muscle-frequency statistic. Do not fix the model to 6.4 Hz without measuring the matched maneuver and retaining counterexamples.
