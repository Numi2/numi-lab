# Measured bird turning and speed: scope and relevance

* Ros et al., 2015, [Pigeons produce aerodynamic torques through changes in wing trajectory during low speed aerial turns](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.104141), [author manuscript](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-6ce6-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content): three pigeons performed level 90-degree turns at center-of-mass speed 3.3 ± 0.2 m/s and wingbeat frequency 8.3 ± 0.3 Hz. The study attributes the observed principal roll and pitch accelerations primarily to aerodynamic torques and reports changes in wing trajectory, not a simple contralateral wingbeat-amplitude explanation. These are pigeon observations, not American-crow measurements and not calibrated targets for this simulation.
* Ros et al., 2011, [Pigeons steer like helicopters and generate down- and upstroke lift during low speed turns](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3250151/): in three pigeons the aerodynamic-force direction averaged across downstrokes was approximately fixed in the body frame during the studied turns; body reorientation redirected it globally. The estimated upstroke impulse was 27 ± 4% of downstroke impulse. The result motivates observing force direction, wingbeat phase, body orientation, and trajectory jointly. Do not confuse the peak-force comparison with the impulse comparison or use pigeon values as crow ground truth.
* Pennycuick, 2001, [Speeds and wingbeat frequencies of migrating birds](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Pennycuick_2001.pdf), Table 2: observed carrion crows (Corvus corone), a related species, had mean sea-level-equivalent airspeed 10.5 ± 2.03 m/s and equivalent wingbeat frequency 3.84 ± 0.0522 Hz (observed quantities, SD, N=6). The paper concerns migrating flight, not slow takeoff or obstacle negotiation, and the airspeed is density corrected; it is not an American-crow target.

The current v15 matched obstacle-free steering diagnostics have a low reference forward speed near 0.35 m/s, while the cited pigeon turns and carrion-crow migration occur in different speed and behavioral regimes. The experiments do not establish that changing speed alone repairs turning. These studies identify a need to measure wing trajectory, phase-resolved aerodynamic force and torque, whole-body motion and air-relative speed before adopting a steering mechanism. Do not claim species realism by matching any single number or scaling pigeon data directly to a crow.
