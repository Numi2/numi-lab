# American crow escape takeoff: direct observations

Jackson and Dial, *Scaling of mechanical power output during burst escape flight in the Corvidae*, Journal of Experimental Biology 214 (2011), 452–461, https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.046789. The paper reports implant measurements for three American crows and describes force-plate takeoff and high-speed recordings during experimentally elicited near-vertical burst escape. It states that the crows (along with gray jays and ravens, unlike the magpies) completed a partial first downstroke before toe-off, and all four species used a pre-liftoff countermovement. This is a qualitative coordination constraint in that particular escape task, not evidence that wings-off takeoff is the normal behavior. Our wings-suppressed matched trial is a causal intervention to establish leg contribution, not a biological template.

The reported American-crow wingbeat frequency after the initial stroke was 6.4 ± 0.1 Hz, maximum 7.1 Hz (table 3). Those values describe the study's selected implanted burst flights; the paper reports values across wingbeats, which must not be treated as three independent estimates. They are not a universal frequency, ordinary cruising target, or a first-stroke target. Body and muscle measurement details, task context and individual variation matter before any numerical comparison.

Jackson's earlier dissertation describes force-plate and high-speed observations across passerines including American crows: https://scholarworks.umt.edu/etd/960/. Its linked full text returned 403 to our reader, so no crow-specific ground-reaction-force or joint-trajectory numbers have been extracted or adopted. The research record and the 2011 paper do not by themselves validate the current simulation. In particular, measure contact impulse, foot release and wing phase from simulated substeps before claiming comparable timing. Preserve the actual takeoff history and do not force a match using animation.
