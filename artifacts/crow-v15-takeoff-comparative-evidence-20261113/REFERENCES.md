# Comparative avian takeoff evidence and applicability

- [Transition from leg to wing forces during take-off in birds](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22972887/) measured perch forces, body and wing kinematics, and airflow in seven zebra finches (~15 g) and three diamond doves (~50 g). The authors report that hindlimbs dominate the initial lift-off velocity and that the start of the first downstroke approximately coincides with foot release. The study reports means and uncertainties for these small perched species; these values are not American-crow thresholds or ground takeoff calibration.
- [Whole-body 3D kinematics of bird take-off: key role of the legs to propel the trunk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29330588/) reports coordinated counter-movement and joint extension, with differences between the two small species. It motivates measuring the whole-body movement rather than assuming the torso is rigid; it does not specify crow joint limits or a simulator controller.
- [Leg thrust important in flight take-off in the pigeon](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/cels_past_depts_facpubs/78/) reports leg-force observations during near-vertical pigeon perch takeoff and describes a wing-up posture at release. It is a comparative constraint, not a crow-specific force or posture calibration.

Research implication: instrument and preserve body center-of-mass motion, full-substep ground impulse, physical wing joint angles and velocity, and first downstroke relative to the last ground contact. Test the leg and flap interventions separately and then together. The current absolute-time flap carrier and zero-amplitude control are engineering probes, not evidence of biological synchronization. Do not impose finch or dove percentage values as crow acceptance gates; seek species-specific data and explicitly retain uncertainty.
