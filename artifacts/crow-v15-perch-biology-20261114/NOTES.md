# Evidence boundaries for avian perch and toe mechanics

Backus and coauthors' comparative mechanical analysis of avian feet ([Royal Society Open Science, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4448815/)) models proximal and distal digit-flexor contributions to grasping and perching. It reports that perching/walking taxa in its comparative sample include corvids and starlings, with relatively larger proximal-flexor force proxies as a group. Its model finds that a single distal flexor can adapt digit shape under contact but often does not reproduce the fully actuated solutions for supporting the bird during perching. The paper supplies pooled normalized geometric assumptions and supplementary measured data; those assumptions are not automatically American-crow-specific forces, moment arms, friction or a validated model for this v15 body.

An experimental study of European starlings ([Galton and Shepherd 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/jez.1714)) reports passive leg flexion alone did not produce toe flexion in anesthetized animals and describes above-perch balancing postures as well as grasping. This is a related passerine, not direct crow motion capture. Avoid assuming ankle flexion automatically locks or closes the toes, and avoid assuming every biologically plausible perch requires the same opposed-toe force pattern. The explicit opposed-toe gate in our present constructed grip-and-release assay is a conservative test of the requested grip, not a universal description of all natural crow perching.

These sources motivate evaluating contact-adaptive proximal and distal actuation and whole-body balance; neither establishes a numeric v15 parameter or validates a new controller. Do not infer a friction coefficient, tendon strength or passive-lock mechanism from these studies without directly traceable measurements and model validation. Retain the failed high-friction, pitch-gate, and toe target experiments.

A primary American-crow morphometry study, [Clark, James and Morari (1991)](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jfo/v062n01/p0132-p0138.pdf), reports Saskatchewan adult tarsus means of 58.9 ± 0.3 mm SE (56 males) and 56.2 ± 0.3 mm SE (46 females). Its tarsus measurement runs from the tibiotarsus–tarsometatarsus joint to the distal end of the unbroken scute over the middle toe. These sample means and standard errors are not distributions of individual birds, do not specify individual toe-segment lengths or tendon properties, and the paper warns of geographic variation. They are a future external morphology comparison, not a basis for changing the v15 toe dimensions or claiming a fit without a mapping from the physical model to the same anatomical landmarks.
